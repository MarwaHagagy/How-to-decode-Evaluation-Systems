# Episode 1: Why I Used to Hate Interviews

Let me begin by saying, I hate interviews. I dreaded ever being asked to do one, and I am the most talkative person you will have the pleasure of meeting in your life. I have the ability to take over any conversation and make it my own (I am that good, my friend). But interviews scare me to death. I feel overwhelmed and can’t talk. The feeling of being judged by what you say is really scary.

**The Moment Everything Changed** 

That was until I started learning qualitative research methods and survey design. I was studying and chatting with my AI companion, discussing how to write surveys, what to add or omit, and how the system works. It mentioned making a pilot study first (interviews) so that we could test our survey questions. Then, in the middle of the discussion, it hit me.

There is an actual science behind interview questions, my friends. They are not out there to intimidate you, or scare you off just for the sake of it; there is a scientific explanation. That is when I realized interviews are not a random collection of questions. They are a measurement instrument.

We were always looking at it from the wrong angle. People always tell us, "When you are presented with X question, answer using Y and Z." Instead, we are going to discuss what each question was designed to test (sorry to disappoint, but it is actually a test!). When you understand the process, you can answer as you see fit with your own words. No more memorizing "best answers" from now on.

So, let’s begin our journey into interview questions with a new twist.

## The Question

> **"Introduce yourself?"** 
> *(Unless you are Elon Musk or someone as important or famous. If you are, I don’t believe you need to read this, my friend, so bye 😊)*

## What the interviewer is actually measuring 

The obvious assumption is that they just want to know you. But don’t they know you already from your C.V., your documents, and the tests you went through? This is the ultimate icebreaker. It is easy enough to answer but hard enough to test your **communication skills and your confidence** in selling yourself. They are measuring how you present your narrative and whether you can make a logical connection between your past work and your future potential.

## Why this construct matters in academia 

Whether you are defending a thesis, presenting at a conference, or networking, you need to be able to pitch your research and your value quickly. The core construct—summarizing complex data (you) into a digestible narrative—is the same in any professional setting.

## Common mistakes

The major mistake is to simply articulate the exact same C.V. they already read. They have the data! You have to be specific and sell yourself. Another mistake is talking so much that you leave no room for a dynamic conversation.

## How to think before answering 

Think of this as the abstract of your research paper or the executive summary of your project. Don't give them the raw data; give them the highlighted narrative. Talk about your most relevant experiences and your most prized projects, but leave room for them to ask questions if they have something on their mind about your C.V.

## Different authentic ways to answer

* **The Highlight Reel:** Focus entirely on the two or three most relevant experiences and prized projects that connect directly to the role you are sitting in front of them for.
* **The "Past, Present, Future" Framework:** Briefly state where you started, what you are building or studying right now, and how this specific position is the logical next step in your data set.

## What a weak vs. strong answer reveals 

* **A weak answer** (reading your C.V. aloud or hesitating) reveals a lack of presentation skills and might flag an issue with the confidence you projected on paper.
* **A strong answer** reveals that you understand your own value proposition, you can control a narrative, and you actually did the work you claimed you did.

## Lessons for future interviews 

This question will be the same in academia, industry, or any other setting, but how you word your accomplishments must differ for every environment. Customize the pitch, but keep the core data the same. 

**"Your answer is the difference, not the question."**
